Deep learning on MRI · November 2025

Brain tumor MRI classification with Grad-CAM

Four classes of brain MRI, and a CNN built from scratch against a fine-tuned EfficientNetB0. In medical imaging a good score proves little on its own, so the project is built around a second question: does the model look at the tumor? Grad-CAM shows where it looks, and a quantitative check against the tumor masks is written and tested.

  • 97.8%test accuracy, fine-tuned EfficientNetB0, against 92.1% for the CNN baseline
  • 0.79 → 0.94recall on meningioma, the hardest class
  • 4 / 4sample heat maps on the region where that type of tumor is expected (qualitative)
  • 1,311held-out test scans
Code and notebook on GitHub

What sets it apart

The model shows where it looks, and I check that it looks at the right place

The stakes. A radiologist cannot rely on a model that answers “glioma, 98%” without showing why. In medical imaging, a good score does not prove that the model learned the disease: it may have learned a shortcut, such as a hospital marker, a type of scanner or a framing habit.

The usual approach

Train a CNN or a pretrained model, report the accuracy and the confusion matrix, and stop there.

Where it breaks. Hundreds of MRI classifiers report 97 to 99% on this kind of dataset. A score that high can come from near-duplicate images shared between training and test, or from a shortcut, and the accuracy alone cannot tell.

What I did differently

  • Grad-CAM, written by hand for both architectures: a heat map of the regions that drove each prediction.
  • The maps checked against anatomy: each type of tumor grows in a typical place, and the maps should find it.
  • A quantitative check against the dataset’s tumor masks (does the hottest point fall in the tumor?), compared with a random map, and a hunt for near-duplicates between training and test.

The proof

97.8%

Test accuracy on 1,311 scans, against 92.1% for the CNN baseline. On one random test scan per class, the heat map lands where that tumor grows: inside the brain tissue for a glioma, against the skull for a meningioma, on the midline at the base of the brain for a pituitary tumor, and nowhere in particular when there is no tumor.

What it does not do. Four maps are an illustration, not a statistic. The quantitative check and the duplicate hunt are written and tested on synthetic data, but have not run on the real dataset yet; the numbers above may drop once duplicates are removed.

What it shows. I do not stop at “it works”: I look for why it works, and whether it is for the right reason.

The problem

Brain tumors look different from one MRI scan to the next, and early detection improves patient outcomes. The goal is a system that recognizes four diagnostic categories, glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor and no tumor, and whose decisions can be inspected.

Disclaimer

Educational and research project. It is not a medical device and must not be used for diagnosis.

The data

The Brain Tumor Dataset on Kaggle: labeled axial MRI slices with variations in acquisition quality, brightness, orientation and anatomy. It also ships tumor segmentation masks, which the quantitative Grad-CAM check uses.

  • Training: 5,712 scans, split 80/20 with stratification into 4,569 for training and 1,143 for validation.
  • Test: 1,311 scans: 300 glioma, 306 meningioma, 405 no tumor, 300 pituitary.

Approach

  • Baseline: custom CNN

    Stacked Conv2D and MaxPooling2D layers, Dropout, a dense head, trained from scratch with Adam. It sets the bar to beat.

  • Transfer learning: EfficientNetB0

    Pretrained on ImageNet, frozen then partially fine-tuned, with a new head: GlobalAveragePooling2D, a dense ReLU layer, dropout and softmax.

Explainability with Grad-CAM

Why it matters here

A clinician has to be able to check a prediction before trusting it, and a score cannot be checked. The risk is concrete: a well-known pneumonia detector trained on chest X-rays from several hospitals had also learned to recognize which hospital an image came from, and partly relied on it (Zech et al., PLOS Medicine, 2018). It scored well, for a reason that would not survive a new hospital.

Regulation points the same way: in the European Union, AI that is part of a medical device is classified as high-risk by the AI Act, with obligations of transparency and human oversight.

How Grad-CAM works

The last convolution layer produces a set of maps, each one picking up a pattern in the image. Grad-CAM measures, through the gradient, how much each map weighed in the score of the predicted class, then adds the maps up with those weights. Laid over the MRI, the result shows in red what tipped the decision.

How to read the four maps

One random test scan per class (seeded), all four correctly predicted by the fine-tuned model. Each type of tumor has a typical location, and the maps find it:

ClassWhere this tumor growsWhat the map shows
GliomaIn the brain tissue itselfA hot spot inside one hemisphere, on the lesion
MeningiomaAt the edge, from the membranes that wrap the brainA hot spot against the skull, at the front
PituitaryOn the midline, at the base of the brainA hot spot centred on the midline, at the base
No tumorNo lesion to findActivation spread over the whole brain, with no focus: the model sees nothing localized

What Grad-CAM does not prove

  • Coarse resolution: the map is a 7 × 7 grid enlarged to the image, so it points at a region, not at a contour.
  • Four examples are not a statistic. They show what the model can do, not how often it does it.
  • The right place is not the whole story: a map on the tumor does not rule out a bias elsewhere in the dataset.

The quantitative check

To turn the illustration into a measure, src/localization.py compares each heat map with the tumor mask shipped with the dataset, on three simple questions, and does the same with a random map as the reference level:

  • Pointing game

    Does the hottest point of the map fall inside the tumor?

  • Energy in the mask

    What share of the map’s total heat lies inside the tumor?

  • Overlap (IoU)

    How much the hot zone and the tumor overlap, at a 0.5 threshold.

Then src/evaluate.py draws the Grad-CAM maps of the model’s most confident errors, which is where explainability helps most: seeing why a meningioma gets taken for something else.

Status

Written and tested on synthetic data, not yet run on the real dataset: running it needs a GPU for a day. The result will read “the hottest point falls in the tumor in X% of test scans, against Y% for a random map”.

Reproducible pipeline

The notebook is the original exploratory work and the source of the results above. The src/ package addresses its weaknesses:

ModulePurpose
dedup.pyPerceptual-hash (dHash) clustering of near-duplicates: a leak-free train/validation split, grouped and stratified, and detection of training images that also appear in the test set
data.pytf.data pipeline with Keras augmentation layers, replacing the deprecated ImageDataGenerator
models.pyCNN baseline and EfficientNetB0; fine-tuning keeps BatchNorm frozen
train.pyTrains either model with the same split, augmentation, class weights and epoch budget, and writes a leakage report
gradcam.pyGrad-CAM for both architectures, also on reloaded models
localization.pyQuantitative Grad-CAM check against tumor masks: pointing game, IoU@0.5, energy in the mask, against a random heat map
evaluate.pyTest metrics with bootstrap 95% intervals, calibration (ECE), top confusions, Grad-CAM gallery of the most confident errors
 
python -m src.train --model cnn      # -> artifacts/cnn.keras
python -m src.train --model tl       # -> artifacts/tl.keras
python -m src.evaluate --model artifacts/tl.keras --seg-dir /path/to/DATASET/segmentation
pytest                               # synthetic-data tests, no dataset needed

Segmentation images do not share file names with the classification ones, so they are matched by content (dHash) to know whether each belongs to the test set, the training set or neither; only the test group gives an unbiased localization score.

Limits and next steps

Possible leakage

Validation accuracy (99.9%) is noticeably higher than test accuracy (97.8%), a sign of near-duplicates shared between training and validation. src/dedup.py measures and removes them; re-running with it is the next step and may lower the reported figures. A lower but clean number is worth more than a fragile 97.8%.

  1. Classification, not segmentation: the model predicts a category; it does not outline the tumor pixel by pixel.
  2. Single run: one seed; with 1,311 test images the 95% interval on accuracy is roughly ±0.8 point.
  3. Real-world MRI: the shift across scanners and protocols is untested; there is no external validation set.
  4. Explainability: the maps on this page are qualitative until src/localization.py runs on the real data.
