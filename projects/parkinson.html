IBM × Probabl hackathon · Kaggle competition · team of three · October 2026

Estimating Parkinson’s motor scores from noisy clinical data

For every patient visit, estimate the motor score the patient would have without medication, from noisy and often missing measurements. In one week and in a team of three, we ran the hackathon as a research lab driven by AI agents, under rules that keep them honest. The submitted blend of five models brings the cross-validated error from 16.51 to 2.51 and placed 7th of 295.

What sets it apart

A research lab run by AI agents, with rules that stop them from cheating

The stakes. In Parkinson’s disease, the “OFF” motor score, measured without the effect of medication, is the one that best tracks how the disease progresses. But getting it means pausing the treatment, so it is rarely measured and often noisy. Estimating it from routine measurements would follow the disease without putting the patient through that. (Synthetic data, built for teaching.)

The usual hackathon

One notebook, one try after another, and whatever lifts the public leaderboard is kept.

Where it breaks. An AI assistant only speeds up that same loop: more tries, more chances of a data leak, and no way to tell a real gain from luck.

What we did differently

  • Analyst agents explore the data and propose hypotheses, which we rank by expected gain and effort.
  • An experimenter agent codes one hypothesis per file.
  • A critic agent reads every costly experiment, looking for a leak, before it runs.
  • A locked evaluation protocol that the agents cannot change scores every run the same way, and a statistical rule decides: a gain smaller than its variability is noise.

The proof

7th / 295

61 experiments in one week, all comparable. Of the 58 compared with a reference, 29 improved the score, 21 were noise and 8 made it worse: those 21 are changes that a comparison of averages could have mistaken for progress. The error is divided by 6.6 (RMSE 16.51 → 2.51).

What it does not do. The data is synthetic: the scores say how well the method works, not that the model is ready for patients.

What it shows. Using AI agents to go fast without giving up rigor, which is the question data teams in companies are asking today.

The problem

In Parkinson’s disease, the MDS-UPDRS motor score depends heavily on levodopa. The OFF score, measured without active medication, says the most about the disease itself, but it is rarely measured, often noisy, and it depends on the rater and on the time since the last dose.

The task: for each visit, predict the true OFF score (the debiased target) from the observed ON and OFF measurements, the levodopa dose, the time since intake and the patient’s history.

  • Metric: RMSE at the visit level.
  • Hold-out: by patient, so no patient appears in both train and test.
  • Format: one week (7 to 14 October 2026), the ESILV × Probabl × IBM “Bobathon” on Kaggle, in a team of three with Bertrand Maigre and Jean-Baptiste Pasteau.

The data

The data is synthetic: 44,590 visits from 5,576 patients for training, 11,013 visits from 1,395 patients for testing. Most columns have holes, and the most useful ones are missing the most often.

Note

The test set contains every visit of each test patient. Previous and next visits, per-patient averages and sequence models are therefore legitimate inputs: they never touch the target.

How the lab worked

The competition required every submission to be produced in Bob, IBM’s agentic IDE, with a skore report. We split the work in two: an exploration lab in Claude Code, where agents look for the best model, and Bob, which re-implements each winning recipe and submits it. Claude explores, Bob produces.

The diagram shows who does what at each stage. The point is where the humans are: we set the rules and make the calls, the agents do the volume, and the protocol is the referee nobody can bribe.

Rules that kept the results honest

  1. Paired decision rule. Each run is compared fold by fold with a reference run. A gain with |Δ| smaller than std(Δ) is noise; a gain below about 0.02 RMSE is logged but does not justify a more complex model.
  2. No leakage. Anything learned from the target (drug response curves, rater bias, encodings, stacking, scalers) is fitted inside fit_predict, on the training folds only, and splits are never made at the visit level.
  3. No tuning on the leaderboard. The public leaderboard was only used to check that it moved with cross-validation.
  4. A critic before costly runs. The leak-reviewer agent is read-only: it can only report, with a verdict per finding (blocking, to check, OK).

A leak the critic stopped

The first big idea was to model the drug: how much a levodopa dose lowers the score as hours pass, with an amplitude per patient. An analyst had fitted the response curve in a scratch script, and the plan was to hard-code its parameters in the experiment.

The leak-reviewer marked it blocking (7 October 2026): that script had fitted the curve using the target of every patient, including the ones that would sit in the validation folds. Hard-coding its numbers would have slipped validation answers into every fold, and the score would have looked better than it was. The fix: refit the curve inside each training fold, from the ON side only.

  • blockedhard-coded curve, fitted on every patient’s target
  • −0.20RMSE once refitted per fold, on 5 folds out of 5 (e004_drug_amplitude)

The idea survived the review and still paid off; it just stopped cheating. That is the job of a critic in an agent loop: agents produce fast, so somebody has to check the work before it counts.

In a company, the same set-up (agents that explore and code, a critic, a protocol they cannot touch) lets a data team test more ideas per week without lowering the bar for what counts as a result.

What worked

The idea that paid the most, in plain words: model how the data is produced rather than pile up features. A score is a patient’s disease level, lowered by the drug depending on the time since the dose, and read by a rater who is more or less severe. Each of those pieces became a feature.

  1. The patient’s context: previous and next visits and per-patient averages take the error from 7.58 to 4.58.
  2. The drug and the rater: a response curve with an amplitude per patient and a scale per rater, down to 4.07.
  3. A latent trajectory per patient, hierarchical then cubic, estimated with a skewed noise per measurement source: 3.23.
  4. The sequence of visits: sequence networks (BiGRU, Transformer) over each patient’s visits, 2.61.
  5. A blend of decorrelated models with non-negative least squares on out-of-fold predictions: 2.51.

Limits

Limits

The data is synthetic, built for teaching. The RMSE figures on this page are out-of-fold cross-validation grouped by patient; the 7th place of 295 is the team’s rank on the competition’s Kaggle leaderboard.

What did not help

Several directions were explored and dropped for lack of a gain above noise: fine per-rater bias, per-patient stacking, pseudo-labeling, heavy network tuning, an MLP alone, an “amortized” Transformer alone. The findings are documented in lab/eda/ and lab/IDEAS.md.

Reproduce

python3 -m venv .venv && .venv/bin/pip install -r requirements.txt -r lab/requirements.txt

# quick test on one fold (not logged)
.venv/bin/python lab/cv.py lab/experiments/e002_hgb_basic_context.py --folds 0

# full run: 5 folds + test predictions, logged in results.csv / LOG.md
.venv/bin/python lab/cv.py lab/experiments/e022b_cubic_traj_quad.py --test --ref e002_hgb_basic_context

# out-of-fold NNLS blend
.venv/bin/python lab/blend.py e023_seq_nn e022b_cubic_traj_quad e025_seq_offset e026_seq_amortized --name b003_seq4

LightGBM needs brew install libomp on macOS (otherwise HistGradientBoostingRegressor is used); the networks use PyTorch. The Kaggle data goes in data/, read-only.

Python, scikit-learn and LightGBM gradient boosting, PyTorch (BiGRU, Transformer), pandas, skore, Claude Code agents, Bob
