Classification on seismic data · October 2025

Tsunami risk from seismic data

Can an earthquake’s seismic characteristics alone say whether it is likely to cause a tsunami? I benchmarked eight classifiers inside leak-proof pipelines, removed a temporal bias that would have leaked the answer, and added a physical feature. The final model is tuned for safety: missing a real tsunami costs far more than a false alarm.

  • 95%of tsunamis detected on the hold-out set (recall)
  • 0.90F1-score on tsunami events, 0.905 in cross-validation
  • 0.90ROC-AUC on the hold-out set
  • 449events kept out of 782, after removing a leak

The problem

The objective is to predict tsunami potential from seismic features only. In this setting the two kinds of error do not cost the same:

ErrorMeaningCost
False positivePredicts a tsunami when none occursInconvenience
False negativeMisses a real tsunamiDisaster: lives at risk

So the model is optimized for recall on tsunami events, not for raw accuracy, and accepts a moderate number of false alarms.

The data

The Global Earthquake–Tsunami Risk Assessment dataset: 782 significant earthquakes recorded worldwide from 2001 to 2022, with magnitude, depth, intensity metrics, location and date, a binary tsunami label (38.9% positive) and no missing values. Tectonic plate boundaries come from the PB2002 model.

A leak in the timeline

Every record from 2001 to 2011 is labeled “no tsunami”, while later years have a realistic mix. A model would learn that the year predicts the answer. I removed the pre-2012 records, and the year and month columns, before any modelling.

Approach

Geospatial feature engineering

A first model reached 95% recall on tsunamis but only 0.62 on non-tsunamis, and it may have relied on indirect location proxies such as latitude, longitude or a Pacific flag. To give it a physical signal instead, I computed distance_to_fault_km: the distance from each epicenter to the nearest tectonic plate boundary, with GeoPandas and Shapely, after projecting both layers to a metric CRS.

Engineered featureIdea
distance_to_fault_kmDistance from the epicenter to the nearest plate boundary (241 boundary segments)
mag_depth_ratioMagnitude divided by depth, which favors strong and shallow quakes
is_shallowDepth of 70 km or less
pacific_regionLongitude in the Pacific ring of fire band
  • Leak-proof pipelines

    Scaling and encoding live inside sklearn.pipeline, so they are fitted on training folds only.

  • Robust validation

    RepeatedStratifiedKFold, 10 folds × 3 repeats: stable estimates on a small, imbalanced dataset.

  • Targeted tuning

    GridSearchCV only on the two best models, gradient boosting and random forest.

Results

Model comparison

Tsunami (61)0.850.950.90

The model correctly classifies about 9 out of 10 earthquakes and detects 19 out of 20 tsunamis. The hold-out F1 (0.90) is close to the cross-validated one (0.905), so there is no sign of overfitting.

Limits

Read 0.90 with care

The hold-out set has only 90 events, 61 of them tsunamis. A model that always answered “tsunami” would already reach a tsunami F1 of 0.81 on that split.

  • Recall on non-tsunami events is 0.66: 10 false alarms out of 29, the price of the recall-first choice.
  • Small dataset: 449 usable events after the leak removal, so every metric has a wide margin of error.
  • Seismic features only: no ocean depth, fault mechanism or sea-level data, which real warning systems rely on.

Reproduce

git clone https://github.com/Victor-MICHELON/Tsunami_Risk_Prediction_Model.git
cd Tsunami_Risk_Prediction_Model
pip install pandas numpy scikit-learn xgboost lightgbm matplotlib seaborn geopandas shapely pyproj
jupyter notebook tsunami-risk-prediction-ml-project-final-version.ipynb

The data files are in Data/. The notebook was written on Kaggle, so its paths point to /kaggle/input/ and need to be adjusted locally.

Python, pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn (Pipeline, GridSearchCV, RepeatedStratifiedKFold), XGBoost, LightGBM, GeoPandas, Shapely, pyproj, Matplotlib, Seaborn
