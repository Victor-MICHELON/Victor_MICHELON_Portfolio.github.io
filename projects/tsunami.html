Classification on seismic data · October 2025

Tsunami risk from seismic data

Can an earthquake’s seismic profile alone say whether it is likely to cause a tsunami? I found and removed a leak that let the model read the answer from the year, benchmarked eight classifiers in leak-proof pipelines, and treated the alarm threshold as a safety decision: missing a real tsunami costs far more than a false alarm.

  • 58 / 61tsunamis detected on the test set, at the default threshold
  • 60 / 61with an alarm threshold chosen in cross-validation, for 2 more false alarms
  • 0.90ROC-AUC on the test set (0.5 = chance)
  • 449events kept out of 782, after removing a leak
Notebook on GitHub Dataset on Kaggle

What sets it apart

A model that cannot cheat, with an alarm set for the error that kills

The stakes. After an earthquake, seismic data arrives within minutes, well before sensors at sea confirm a wave. A reliable first signal from that data alone buys time. And the two errors do not cost the same: a false alarm disrupts, a missed tsunami kills.

The usual approach

Train a classifier on the dataset as it is, and maximize overall accuracy.

Where it breaks. In this dataset, every earthquake from 2001 to 2011 is labeled “no tsunami”: the model learns that the year gives the answer. Overall accuracy then treats a missed tsunami like a false alarm, and latitude and longitude let the model memorize regions rather than physics.

What I did differently

  • Found and removed the leak before any modelling: 449 earthquakes kept out of 782, year and month dropped.
  • Judged the models on the tsunami class, not on overall accuracy, then chose the alarm threshold on the training folds to keep at least 98% of tsunamis.
  • Added a physical feature, the distance from the epicenter to the nearest plate boundary, and measured whether it helps.

The proof

60 / 61

At the default threshold, the model detects 58 of the 61 tsunamis in the test set, with 10 false alarms out of 29 quiet earthquakes. With the threshold chosen on the training folds, without looking at the test set, it misses 1 tsunami instead of 3, for 12 false alarms instead of 10. With the leak left in, the same model would have shown 93.6% accuracy and 0.98 ROC-AUC instead of 85.6% and 0.90: a better-looking score, worthless on a new earthquake.

What it does not do. The physical feature did not measurably help: cross-validated F1 is 0.905 with it and 0.897 without, within noise. And tuning for overall accuracy selects the very same model as tuning for the tsunami class: the metric alone changed nothing, the threshold did.

What it shows. I read the data before modelling it, I choose the metric and the threshold from the real cost of errors, and I report the ideas that did not pay.

The problem

The objective is to predict tsunami potential from seismic features only. In this setting the two kinds of error do not cost the same:

ErrorMeaningCost
False positivePredicts a tsunami when none occursDisruption
False negativeMisses a real tsunamiDisaster: lives at risk

So the models are compared on the tsunami class rather than on raw accuracy, and the final alarm threshold accepts a moderate number of false alarms to miss as few tsunamis as possible.

The data

The Global Earthquake–Tsunami Risk Assessment dataset: 782 significant earthquakes recorded worldwide from 2001 to 2022, with magnitude, depth, intensity metrics, location and date, a binary tsunami label (38.9% positive) and no missing values. Tectonic plate boundaries come from the PB2002 model.

A leak in the timeline

Every record from 2001 to 2011 is labeled “no tsunami”, while later years have a realistic mix. A model would learn that the year predicts the answer. I removed the pre-2012 records, and the year and month columns, before any modelling.

Approach

Geospatial feature engineering

A first model might rely on indirect location proxies such as latitude, longitude or a Pacific flag. To give it a physical signal instead, I computed distance_to_fault_km: the distance from each epicenter to the nearest tectonic plate boundary, with GeoPandas and Shapely, after projecting both layers to a metric coordinate system. Whether it helped is measured further down.

Engineered featureIdea
distance_to_fault_kmDistance from the epicenter to the nearest plate boundary (241 boundary segments)
mag_depth_ratioMagnitude divided by depth, which favors strong and shallow quakes
is_shallowDepth of 70 km or less
pacific_regionLongitude in the Pacific ring of fire band
  • Leak-proof pipelines

    Scaling and encoding live inside sklearn.pipeline, so they are fitted on training folds only.

  • Robust validation

    RepeatedStratifiedKFold, 10 folds × 3 repeats: stable estimates on a small, imbalanced dataset.

  • Targeted tuning

    GridSearchCV only on the two best models, gradient boosting and random forest.

  • The leak would have flattered the model: 93.6% accuracy and 0.98 ROC-AUC, because the test set would then contain 2001 to 2011 earthquakes that the year alone classifies. Nothing of that carries over to a new earthquake.
  • The threshold is the safety lever. Chosen on out-of-fold predictions of the training set, to keep at least 98% of tsunamis, it misses one tsunami instead of three for two more false alarms, at the same overall accuracy.
  • The physical feature did not pay. Without it, cross-validated F1 drops by 0.008, within noise, and the test set even looks slightly better. I keep it for interpretability, not for the score.

Limits

Read 0.90 with care

The test set has only 90 events, 61 of them tsunamis. A model that always answered “tsunami” would already reach a tsunami F1 of 0.81 on that split, which is why the counts of missed tsunamis and false alarms say more than the F1.

  • False alarms are the price: 10 to 12 out of 29 quiet earthquakes, depending on the threshold.
  • Small dataset: 449 usable events after the leak removal, so every metric has a wide margin of error, and one tsunami more or less moves recall by 1.6 points.
  • Seismic features only: no ocean depth, fault mechanism or sea-level data, which real warning systems rely on.
